Two images of the same size are compared pixel by pixel. For each position the comparator asks: are these two colors "the same"? Then it counts the positions where the answer is no, draws them in red and gives you exactly this as a result: a diff image and a mismatch count. Everything a tool adds on top is about making that comparisons less naive.

## Color distance

The first refinement is that `rgb(120, 120, 120)` and `rgb(121, 120, 120)` are not a difference anyone can see. Comparators compute a distance between the two colors and treat anything under a per-pixel color tolerance as equal. [pixelmatch](https://github.com/mapbox/pixelmatch), the engine inside Playwright, Vitest, `jest-image-snapshot` and `@pixsame/cypress-plugin-visual-regression-diff`, converts to the YIQ color space, where luminance differences weigh more than hue differences, because that matches how eyes work. [looks-same](https://github.com/gemini-testing/looks-same) uses CIEDE2000, a perceptual color difference formula. [Resemble.js](https://github.com/rsmbl/Resemble.js), behind BackstopJS, offers tolerances per channel.

The color tolerance is the `threshold` option in pixelmatch-based tools (0 to 1, default 0.1) and the "ignore colors" or "ignore less" modes in Resemble. Raising it hides subtle color shifts, lowering it makes anti-aliasing visible. The number only means something on the scale of the algorithm that produced it: a `0.1` in pixelmatch's YIQ space is not a `0.1` anywhere else. That is why the [manifest](/manifest) records it as `comparison.colorThreshold` together with `comparison.colorMetric` (`yiq`, `resemble`, `deltaE` or your own string), and leaves both out when the writer does not know them.

## Anti-aliasing

Text and diagonal edges are drawn with partially transparent pixels along their border and the exact outcome depends on the font engine, the GPU and the browser version. Two renders of the same text can differ in hundreds of pixels that no one can tell apart. `pixelmatch` detects likely anti-aliased pixels (a pixel that sits between two very different neighbors) and ignores them by default. `odiff` and `looks-same` do the same. Without it, pixel-level comparison would be unusable for text-heavy UIs.

::tip
To dive deeper into the topic of anti-aliasing we recommend great introductory piece written by [Paul Lewis](https://web.dev/articles/antialiasing-101): https://web.dev/articles/antialiasing-101
::

## The maximum diff ratio

After per-pixel decisions the tool has a count of different pixels. The second limit says how many are allowed before the comparison fails: as an absolute number (`maxDiffPixels` in Playwright, `allowedMismatchedPixels` in Vitest, `failureThreshold` with `failureThresholdType: 'pixel'` in jest-image-snapshot) or as a share of all pixels (`maxDiffPixelRatio`, `allowedMismatchedPixelRatio`, `errorThreshold`, `misMatchThreshold` in percent for BackstopJS, `maxDiffThreshold` in `@pixsame/cypress-plugin-visual-regression-diff`).

A ratio is portable across viewports, a pixel count is not: 100 pixels is a lot in a 200×100 icon and nothing in a full page. Most teams end up with a small ratio (0.1% to 1%) and a handful of exceptions.

The manifest stores this limit as one number, `comparison.maxDiffRatio`, next to the measured `comparison.diffRatio`. A pixel count is converted to a ratio of the image size, and when a tool is configured with both a count and a ratio, the stricter one is stored. A writer that does not know the limit leaves the field out, which is not the same as a tolerance of zero. The outcome that counts is always the entry's `status`: a ratio computed from a count can disagree with the tool by a rounding error at the boundary.

## Try the two limits

Two renders of the same screen, compared with pixelmatch's rule in your browser. Turn the changes on and off, then move the two limits. Anti-aliasing detection takes away about half of the pixels the sub-pixel text shift produces (the rest is [chapter 3](/learn/why-screenshots-flake)'s problem). The button tint vanishes once the color tolerance passes 0.15. The price change is a few dozen pixels, so raising the maximum diff ratio to 0.1% lets it through.

But is it what we wanted to achieve? Try it by yourself: what would be ideal color threshold and max diff ratio values? Is there one configuration that fits every case?

:demo-pixel-diff

::collapsible{name="the solutions" openText="Show" closeText="Hide"}
**Typography errors.** Set the color tolerance to about 0.45 and the maximum diff ratio to 0.05%. A small typo changes only a few pixels, so the limits have to be tight, and that is only possible once every drawing and anti-aliasing difference is filtered out. Otherwise those pixels bury the real issue.

**Background coloring issues.** These are bigger, graphical mistakes. A color tolerance around 0.10 with a maximum diff ratio close to 2% works: every change that covers a large area is caught, while small color distances still count as differences.

**Rasterizer and anti-aliasing differences.** These are the hardest to catch, because they grow with the size of the image and the amount of text on the page, so no single configuration fits. The only real solution is a comparison tool that filters them out automatically.
::

This is why the good comparison algorithm helps so much when doing visual testing. It's hard to cover all bases with only simple pixel-to-pixel comparator.

## Dimension mismatch

If the actual image has a different size, there is nothing to compare. Tools either fail outright (Playwright, with a message about the expected size) or compare the overlapping region and report the difference (Resemble reports `isSameDimensions` and the delta). A size change usually means content moved below the fold or a scrollbar appeared - it is a real finding.

## Perceptual comparison

Pixel comparison with anti-aliasing detection is good enough for most UIs. Where it is not, the alternatives are:

- SSIM, the [structural similarity index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_similarity_index_measure), compares local patterns of luminance, contrast and structure rather than single pixels. jest-image-snapshot offers it as `comparisonMethod: 'ssim'` - it is more tolerant of compression and sub-pixel shifts, but slower.
- Ignore regions: mask areas that are expected to change (a clock, an avatar, an ad slot). Playwright's `mask` option, odiff's ignore regions and the stylesheet trick (inject CSS that hides the element before the screenshot) all do this.
- Hosted services (Pixsame, Applitools, Percy, Chromatic) run their own comparison on the server, often with layout-aware or AI-assisted modes that try to tell "the text moved" from "the text changed".

## Reading a diff image

Most tools draw differing pixels in a highlight color over a faded copy of the baseline. `jest-image-snapshot` and Roborazzi write a three-panel composite (baseline, diff, actual) in one file. Playwright writes three separate files and shows them with a slider in its report. When you build your own review step, a slider between baseline and actual is what reviewers reach for first. The diff image shines in other place- it's good for finding the one changed pixel in a sea of same.
